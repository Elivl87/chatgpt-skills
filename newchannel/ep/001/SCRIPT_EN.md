## THESIS (why this is not CGP Grey)

**"A traffic jam isn't a place. It's a wave. And one patient driver can absorb it."**

- CGP Grey's video (44.6M) explains "traffic snakes" with animation and argues for a rule *every* car must follow (keep the
  same distance in front and behind) and, in the end, for self-driving cars (per press/blog summaries of the video; we
  did not re-watch it, on purpose, to avoid copying its structure). The Sugiyama clip (4.3M) shows *that* a jam
  forms. Both stop at "humans are the problem".
- We go further, with **four real experiments in one story** that no big video ties together: the 2008 ring (the jam is
  real), the 2009 Nagoya Dome run (the **tipping point**: 25 cars flow, 28 jam), the US ring where **one car out of 21**
  erased the wave (and a **human driver** did it too, with one number: the average speed), and the 2022 **100-car highway
  test** near Nashville, told honestly: messy, and with one unforgettable moment where the wave shrinks, grows, and shrinks
  again depending on who drives through it.
- The payoff is **actionable for one person, today**, not "wait for robots": leave a safe gap, drive at the speed traffic
  is actually moving. And a reframing people repeat: *the driver who caused your jam left half an hour ago.*
- Visual difference: our dots are a live, sonified simulation in print style, not cartoon cars; the sim is re-run on
  screen for each experiment (same ring, different rule) instead of stock footage.

## Narration-only text (for voice and word count)

Twenty-two cars. One circle. And one driver who taps the brakes. Just once. Just a little. Now watch. Everybody behind
brakes harder… and harder… until they stop. No crash. No roadworks. No traffic light. Nothing in the way. And look at the
jam. It's moving backwards. Our dot is sure it didn't do anything wrong. Nobody did. So why do traffic jams appear out of
nowhere?

First: this isn't just a cartoon. In 2008, a team of physicists in Japan did this with real cars. Twenty-two of them, on
a circle two hundred and thirty metres around. The drivers had one job: drive safely, at about thirty kilometres an hour.
For a while, it worked. Then tiny wobbles crept in. A gap opens here, a squeeze there. About three minutes in: five cars
stopped dead. On an empty circle. With nothing in the way. And the jam did the creepy thing. Every car kept driving
forward, but the jam slid backwards, at about twenty kilometres an hour. Roughly the speed of a bicycle. The same speed
jams move on real highways.

So let's slow it down and unroll the circle into a straight line. Driver one taps the brakes. Driver two notices a moment
later, and now the gap is smaller, so they brake a bit harder. Driver three is later still, and brakes harder still. By
car ten: a full stop. Engineers call this string instability. The string is just the line of cars. In a stable string, a
wobble fades as it travels back. In an unstable one, every driver adds a little extra, like a whisper that gets louder
each time it's passed on. And here's why it travels backwards. The jam isn't made of cars. Cars drive into the back of
it, crawl through, and leave from the front. The cars move on. The jam stays a jam. It's a wave, like the wave in a
stadium: nobody leaves their seat, but the wave goes all the way around. A traffic jam isn't a place. It's a wave. But
not every circle jams. In a bigger follow-up, the same researchers borrowed a baseball stadium, the Nagoya Dome, and drew
a circle three hundred and fourteen metres around. With twenty-five cars, traffic flowed. With twenty-eight or more, the
jams came. Three extra cars. Below a certain crowding, wobbles fade. Above it, they grow. Now scale that up to a highway
at rush hour. A single wave can roll backwards for more than ten kilometres, at fifteen or twenty kilometres an hour. So
the jam you're stuck in right now? The brake tap that started it may have happened half an hour ago. That driver is long
gone. Probably home. Probably eating dinner.

So how do you stop a wave? You'd think you'd need every driver to change. Or a road full of robot cars. A few years
later, a team in the US rebuilt the circle with twenty-one cars, and told the drivers to drive like it's rush hour: if a
gap opens, close it. One of those cars was different. A computer ran its speed, with one simple idea: don't chase the car
ahead. Drive at the average speed of traffic. Let the gap in front grow and shrink. When the jam ahead brakes, you're
already slowing gently. When it clears, you're not racing to catch up. The wave melted. Speed swings around the ring
dropped by about eighty percent. Hard braking almost vanished. On that little track, the cars burned up to about forty
percent less fuel. And more cars got around per hour, not fewer. One car out of twenty-one. Then they set the computer a
little faster than the average. And the wave came back. Patience isn't driving slowly. It's driving at the average. But
here's my favourite part. In another run, there was no computer at all. Just a trained driver, given one number: the
average speed. Speed swings roughly halved. Fuel dropped by about a fifth. One patient human. Why does it work? Because a
gap is a shock absorber. The patient car reaches the jam late, after it has started to clear, so it never has to stop.
Everyone behind it never feels the wave. Researchers in Japan worked out the theory and gave it a wonderful name:
jam-absorption driving. It's not magic, though. If a road is simply overfull, no driver can absorb that.

So can you do it on a real highway? In November 2022, a team sent one hundred cars with a smart cruise control into
rush-hour traffic near Nashville, Tennessee. The biggest test of its kind. And the honest answer is: it's messy. A
hundred cars is a drop in a highway. They didn't erase every wave, and they didn't expect to. But in the data there's
one moment I keep thinking about. A wave rolls back down the highway. Two smart cars drive through it, and it shrinks.
Four cars with the system switched off go through, and it grows. More smart cars, and it shrinks again. The wave
remembers how everyone drove through it.

Which means you're in the wave too. Every time. So leave a gap: a safe one, not a giant one. Don't race to close it.
Drive at the speed traffic is actually moving, not the speed you wish it was. You won't get there later. Same distance,
same time, less braking. And somewhere behind you, a jam that never happens. One tap of the brakes made this jam. One
patient driver can unmake it. This is Ten Thousand Dots. Tiny systems, explained with dots.
