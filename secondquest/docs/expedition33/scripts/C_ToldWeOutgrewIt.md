# Expedition 33 · Package C · "The Game We Were Told We'd Outgrown" (nostalgia → legacy → letting go)

Status: **DRAFT v2** (after the 4-lens review, see `../REVIEW.md`). Not locked. Episode number set by the Producer.
Factual basis: `../RESEARCH.md` (ids in the notes at the end).

## Creative spine
- **A. Viewer question:** Why did millions fall for a turn-based RPG, the kind of game the industry spent a decade
  treating as something we'd outgrown?
- **B. Hidden question:** What do you do with the things you loved when you were young, and the people who made them,
  once they're gone?
- **C. Surface attraction:** an underdog (a team of about thirty, a Reddit post, a SoundCloud composer) that beat the
  giants; the parry system; the music.
- **D. Deeper desire:** permission to still love what we loved, without having to live in the past.
- **E. Thesis:** Expedition 33 is a game about a family that can't leave a painting their dead son made, made by
  a man who never left the games of his childhood. It lands because it gives both the same answer: you honour what
  you lost not by living inside it, but by letting it end and painting something new for someone else.
- **F. Payoff:** the motto that sent expeditions to die "for those who come after" becomes what every maker, and every
  mourner, finally has to do: let go, and leave something for the next ones.
- **G. Risk:** an industry/news essay (the EP002 lesson); "turn-based is back" hot take; underdog cliché.
- **H. Recognition anchors:** the menu (Attack / Skill / Item), the parry, FF VIII, the Reddit post, the SoundCloud
  composer, Game of the Year everywhere, the painting reveal, Maelle vs Verso.
- **I. Humour:** "the monster waits politely for its turn"; "don't call it turn-based"; "not how you staff a Game
  of the Year; it is how you start a band"; "Sekiro... the game famous for making adults cry, for different reasons".
- **J. Why SecondQuest:** the audience *is* this story: adults who grew up with these games and play less now.

## Master script (EN) · narration

### A · Hook · spoiler-free
> VISUAL: a battle screen in our style: Quest on the left, a monster on the right, waiting with its arms folded. A menu: ATTACK / SKILL / ITEM. The cursor blinks.

There's a kind of game a lot of us grew up with. You pick "Attack" from a menu. And the monster waits... politely... for its turn.

For about ten years, the industry treated that kind of game like something we'd grown out of.

Then a team of about thirty people in the south of France made one anyway.

It won Game of the Year at every major awards show.

And one of the most respected executives in PlayStation history had given them one piece of advice. Whatever you do... don't call it turn-based.

So, why?

> VISUAL: wordmark + gold bar; the companion. 1.5 s silence. Card: FULL SPOILERS · INCLUDING THE ENDING.

### W · Warning

One warning. To answer that, we have to go all the way to the ending. If you haven't finished it... save your game. Then come back.

### B · The boy who couldn't read
> VISUAL: a living room, late 1990s, our own drawing. Two small brothers on the carpet in front of a big CRT. On screen, a wall of text neither can read. A tower; a boss; GAME OVER.

Start with a kid.

The man who directed Clair Obscur: Expedition 33 is Guillaume Broche. His first Final Fantasy was the eighth one. He has said he played it as a child, when neither he nor his brother could read yet.

They couldn't follow a single line of the story. They played it anyway. And what he remembers most is losing to the first real boss, at the top of a tower.

He still calls it the best one. In his words: "I will die on that hill."

Then the kid grows up. He works at Ubisoft, one of the biggest game companies in the world. And what he wants to play... is that. A game with menus and turns, but made the way games look now.

Nobody is making it. So in twenty twenty, during lockdown, he posts on Reddit. He's looking for voice actors for a demo.

One of the first replies comes from a writer in Australia who has never done voice acting. She ends up as the game's lead writer.

The composer was a guitar teacher, posting his music on SoundCloud.

This is not how you staff a Game of the Year. It is, however, exactly how you start a band.

### C · The trail
> VISUAL: the black tower over the sea; a brush paints "33". The harbor; volunteers board; on the hull, hand-lettered: FOR THOSE WHO COME AFTER. Then the path inland: along it, small stone cairns shaped like old menu boxes and cartridge labels (our own drawings, no logos).

What they built is a world on a deadline.

Once a year, a giant figure called the Paintress paints a number on a stone tower. Everyone that age or older turns to petals and is gone. This year, the number is thirty-three.

And every year, the people with one year left cross the sea to stop her. Every expedition before has failed. They go anyway, with a motto.

"For those who come after."

We probably won't make it. But we can leave the trail a little clearer for the next ones.

Which is a funny thing for this team to build. Because they were walking a trail too.

Final Fantasy. The games Broche grew up on. The ones that, for a decade, the big studios had been quietly leaving behind for action combat.

Every one of those games was a note on the road. This one picked them up.

### D · The menu that needs your hands
> VISUAL: the battle screen again. The monster swings; Quest presses a button at the last frame: a gold flash, PARRY. The monster looks offended.

But it didn't just copy them.

You still choose from a menu. You still take turns. But when the enemy attacks, you don't just watch. You dodge. You parry. In real time, with your own hands.

They borrowed it from Sekiro, an action game famous for making adults cry... for very different reasons.

So the monster still waits for its turn. It just doesn't wait for you anymore.

That's the trick. It kept the feeling and fixed the waiting.

And it changes what the menu means. In the old games, the menu was where you rested. Here, choosing "Attack" is a promise. In two seconds, something is going to swing at you... and you'd better be paying attention.

Because it turns out we never outgrew menus. We outgrew sitting still. A lot of us were never bored of these games. We were busy. Tired. Older.

And here was one that met us where we are now.

### E · What the world really is · spoilers start
> VISUAL: the camera pulls back from Lumière until it is a canvas on an easel in a Paris studio around 1900. Toys of a grown son on a shelf: a small wooden boat, a sketchbook.

But mechanics don't make millions of people cry. The story does. And the story is about the same thing, in a way nobody expected.

Deep in the game, you learn that this whole world is a painting.

In the real world, there's a family of painters called the Dessendres. They can paint worlds and walk inside them. Their son, Verso, painted this one.

Then he died in a fire.

His mother couldn't let go. She climbed into his painting, the last thing of him that was still alive, and she stayed. She is the Paintress.

His father went in after her. To bring her home, he started erasing it. That's the countdown. That's the petals.

So the whole world of Expedition 33 is a family that can't leave something their son made... because leaving it means admitting he's gone.

And the strangest part is the father.

For most of the game, whatever is erasing Lumière feels like pure evil. A number on a tower. A city turning to petals, one birthday at a time.

Then you learn who's doing it, and why. He isn't destroying a world for fun. He's trying to get his wife out of a room she won't leave. He's the one person in the family willing to say the cruelest true thing out loud.

You can't live in there.

The monster of this story... is the stage of grief nobody wants to be in.

### F · Living inside a painting
> VISUAL: a grown-up's bedroom, today: on the shelf, an old console, a stack of games, a memory card. The light from the window is the same light as in the Paris studio.

And here's where it gets uncomfortable. Because every one of us has a painting like that.

A world somebody made, years ago, that we lived inside for a while. A save file we never deleted. A soundtrack we still hum without noticing. Sometimes, the people who made it aren't even around anymore.

At the end of Expedition 33, the game makes you choose.

Maelle, the youngest of the family, reborn inside the painting, wants to keep it. Bring everyone back. Stay.

But she was never meant to live in there. The longer she stays, the weaker she gets.

Or Verso's way. The painted son. He wants it to end. Let the painting go, so his family can finally go home.

That's a strange thing for a video game to say to people who love video games. You can visit the painting. You can love it forever. But you can't live in it.

### G · What you do instead
> VISUAL: a modern studio corner: a small team at desks (silhouettes, our own designs), one easel among the monitors with a blank canvas.

And that's what makes the story behind the game fit so well.

Broche didn't remake Final Fantasy Eight. He didn't climb into it and refuse to come out. He took what it gave him... and made something new, for somebody else.

And they're doing it again. Asked about their next game, Broche has called it "a bit crazy." They aren't moving into their own painting either.

And now it's happening to other people too. Publishers are reportedly getting a steady stream of pitches for turn-based RPGs. Somewhere right now, a team nobody has heard of is opening a blank file because of this game.

And somewhere, someone is playing Expedition 33 as their first one of these. Maybe not understanding all of it yet. The way a kid once played a game he couldn't read.

### H · Payoff + CTA
> VISUAL: the Paris studio, morning. The big painting is covered with a cloth. Next to it, an empty easel with a fresh, blank canvas and a brush laid across it. The motto is painted along the easel's foot, white: FOR THOSE WHO COME AFTER. The companion sits on the brush.

So... why did millions love the kind of game we were told we'd outgrown?

Because we never outgrew it. We just lost it for a while.

A son paints a world, and dies. His family can't leave it. And in the end, the kindest thing anyone can do with what he made... is let it end, so they can go home.

And a kid who once couldn't read grows up, and paints a new one.

For those who come after.

> VISUAL: 1 s of silence. End card; the companion goes into the wordmark (book-end of the hook).

So which ending did you choose? And what game deserves a "why?" next? Tell me in the comments.

Maybe that's our next quest.
