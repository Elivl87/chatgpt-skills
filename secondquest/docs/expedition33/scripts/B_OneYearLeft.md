# Expedition 33 · Package B · "One Year Left" (the deadline → what we leave behind)

Status: **DRAFT v2** (after the 4-lens review, see `../REVIEW.md`). Not locked. Episode number set by the Producer.
Factual basis: `../RESEARCH.md` (ids in the notes at the end).

## Creative spine
- **A. Viewer question:** Why did millions spend thirty hours of their own free time on a game about running out of time?
- **B. Hidden question:** If you knew your number, what would you owe the people who stay?
- **C. Surface attraction:** a brilliant premise (a public death-clock), a beautiful world, a famous Act 1 shock.
- **D. Deeper desire:** to believe a short life still counts, and that the people we lose would want us to keep going.
- **E. Thesis:** the Gommage makes everyone's deadline public, so the game can ask what a deadline is *for*. Its
  answer: not beating the clock, but what you leave on the road. Then the twist shows the clock itself was grief, a
  family that couldn't let go, and the last thing a dying man leaves behind is permission to live after him.
- **F. Payoff:** the motto, a soldier's creed in the first hour, becomes a dead man's permission: go on without me.
- **G. Risk:** a "memento mori" TED talk; a plot recap; preaching about time management.
- **H. Recognition anchors:** the number, the arithmetic, Gustave's last year, the journals (Expedition 60's muscles),
  the cliff, "the new guy", Maelle's choice.
- **I. Humour:** the arithmetic ("you don't have thirteen years, you have about seven"); "a city of extremely punctual
  people"; the muscles journal; "a very French way to spend a final year".
- **J. Why SecondQuest:** the strange fact (a public death-clock you spend your evenings in) under a human truth (we
  all have a number; the question is what we leave).

"spend your time for the next ones" to "the next ones must be allowed to stop grieving and live". It is grief,
legacy and letting go in one line, and it lands on the survivor walking *away* from the harbor, not toward it.

### A · Hook · spoiler-free
> VISUAL: the sea at dawn; the black tower; a giant brush paints "33". Cut to a street clock, a calendar, a child's height chart on a door frame, all quietly showing the same number.

Imagine knowing exactly how much time you have left.

Not roughly. Exactly. It's painted on a tower across the sea, big enough for the whole city to read.

This year, it says thirty-three. Next year, thirty-two.

Everyone in this city knows the year they'll disappear.

And millions of people chose to spend thirty hours of their own free time... inside a game about running out of it.

So, why?

### W · Warning

### B1 · The arithmetic
> VISUAL: a café table in Lumière; Quest does the sum on a napkin with a pencil. Two lines approach each other: "AGE" climbing, "NUMBER" falling.

The game is called Clair Obscur: Expedition 33. And its whole world runs on one rule.

Once a year, a giant figure called the Paintress paints a number on a stone tower. Everyone that age or older turns to petals and is gone. It's called the Gommage.

And every year, the number goes down by one.

Which sounds simple, until you do the math. Because while the number goes down, you go up.

If you're twenty, you don't have thirteen years left. You have about seven.

Every birthday is a step forward. Every painting is a step back. The gap closes from both ends.

So everybody in the city of Lumière knows their year. Not roughly. To the day.

Which makes it a city of extremely punctual people.

And a city with no old people at all. No grandparents telling the same story twice. No one to ask what it was like before. In Lumière, the oldest person you know is always the next to go.

### B2 · What do you do with a year?
> VISUAL: the harbor; volunteers in long coats board a boat. On the hull, hand-lettered in white: FOR THOSE WHO COME AFTER.

So, what do you do with your last year?

Lumière's answer is strange and very brave. The people with one year left volunteer for an expedition. They cross the sea and try to kill the Paintress before she paints again.

Every expedition before this one has failed. Every single one.

And they go anyway. They even have a motto for it. They say it as the boats leave.

"For those who come after."

Meaning: we probably won't make it. But maybe we can leave the road a little clearer for the next ones.

One of them is Gustave. He's an engineer. He has one year left. He could spend it in a café, with a good coat and a terrible view of the tower.

Instead, he gets on the boat. Which is a very Belle Époque way to spend a final year. Dramatic. Well dressed. Doomed.

The game even opens on his clock. Before he leaves, he walks a woman he once loved down to the harbor. Her name is Sophie. She's thirty-three. He gets one last minute with her.

Then the petals. Then the boat.

### B3 · The road behind you
> VISUAL: a cliff path; a small glowing journal lies on a rock. Quest kneels and opens it. Scraps of handwriting drift up from it (our own lettering).

And the road really is full of people who came before.

You keep finding their journals. Dozens of expeditions, every year back to the beginning. Their maps. Their warnings. Their last entries.

Some are heartbreaking. Some are... not.

One expedition's journal reads, word for word: "Bare naked muscles will save the world!"

They did not save the world.

But they wrote it down. And sixty years of strangers left you a trail. Every note is someone who ran out of time... and spent the last of it on you.

That's the first answer the game gives. A deadline isn't only something you lose. It's something you can give away.

### C · The cliff · spoilers start
> VISUAL: night, the cliff above the sea. A girl in a red-trimmed coat (our design) behind Quest. Cut before any blow: the coat on the rocks; then the girl alone, holding his satchel.

But the game doesn't let that stay abstract.

On the first night across the sea, most of Expedition 33 is killed.

And at the end of the first act, Gustave, the man you've played since the opening, dies too. Protecting the youngest of them. A sixteen-year-old named Maelle.

His last words to her are the motto.

"For those who come after."

It's not about the next expedition anymore. It's about her.

And then the game does the hardest, most ordinary thing it can. It keeps going. A stranger takes his place in your party. The lead writer has said one actor's charisma did some "heavy lifting" to get players to accept him.

Because that's what a deadline looks like from the other side. Someone runs out of time. And everyone else... still has a Tuesday.

### D · Who's holding the clock
> VISUAL: the camera pulls back from Lumière until it is a canvas on an easel in a Paris studio around 1900. A giant rag wipes one corner; a giant brush paints it back, more slowly each time.

And then the game tells you what the clock really is.

This whole world is a painting.

In the real world, a family of painters called the Dessendres can paint worlds and walk inside them. Their son, Verso, painted this one. Then he died in a fire.

His mother couldn't let go. She climbed into his painting. She is the Paintress.

His father went in after her, to bring her home. And to bring her home, he started erasing the painting.

That's the Gommage. Every number on that tower, every petal, every failed expedition. A father erasing his son's last painting. And a mother slowing him down, one year at a time.

So the deadline was never fate. It was grief. A family that couldn't agree on how long to hold on.

Everyone in Lumière was living on borrowed time. Borrowed... from someone else's mourning.

### E · Maelle's year
> VISUAL: the girl from the cliff, older now in the light, standing at an easel with a brush in her hand. Behind her, a full city, lamps lit.

And the girl from the cliff? She's part of that family too.

Maelle is the youngest Dessendre, Alicia, reborn inside the painting with no memory of who she was. Out there, the fire scarred her. In here, it never touched her. In here, she had friends. A home. A man who died for her.

And the stranger who took Gustave's place? He's the painted version of the dead son. Verso. Held together by the last little piece of the real one.

He was on the very first expedition, decades ago, and he never got older. He has watched expedition after expedition arrive... and not one of them go home. He has outlived everyone he ever walked with.

In a world where everyone is terrified of their number, he is the one person who wants his to come.

Because a clock that never runs out... isn't a gift. It's just a longer goodbye.

So at the very end, she holds the brush. And she can do what nobody in Lumière ever could.

She can stop the clock. Bring everyone back. Everyone she lost. All of them, home.

But she was never meant to live in there. The longer she stays, the weaker she gets.

Or she can let it go. The painting ends. And she goes back to a real life, with a real family, standing at a real grave.

The game makes you choose. It doesn't tell you which one is right.

Either way, someone runs out of time. The only question is who.

### F · Why it lands
> VISUAL: Lumière's street from block B1, now at evening; the napkin with the sum, abandoned on the café table. The companion sits on it.

And that's why it hits so hard. It isn't really a game about dying.

Everyone in it is going to run out of time. That was never the question. The question was what they do with it, and who they do it for.

Nobody paints our number on a tower. Which is probably the kindest thing the universe does for us.

But most of us have stood on that cliff from the other side. Somebody ran out of time... and we were the ones still standing there. Holding their bag.

### G · Payoff + CTA
> VISUAL: the harbor, morning. A boat pulls away; FOR THOSE WHO COME AFTER on its hull. The girl from the cliff (from behind) is not on it: she turns and walks back up the street into the city, toward the lit cafés. The companion goes with her.

So... why did millions spend thirty hours on a game about running out of time?

Because it isn't about the time you have.

It's about what the people you lose leave in your hands.

In the first hour, the motto is what you say to a boat.

By the end, it's what someone says to you, so you don't have to follow them.

It's permission.

For those who come after.

> VISUAL: 1 s of silence. End card; the companion goes into the wordmark (book-end of the hook).

So, what would you do with your last year? And what game deserves a "why?" next? Tell me in the comments.

Maybe that's our next quest.
