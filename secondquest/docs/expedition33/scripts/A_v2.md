## What changed from v1, and what stayed
- **Kept:** the packaging (title and thumbnail), the spoiler-free hook (one fact corrected: "eight million copies", not
  "people"), the "So, why?" wordmark beat with Esquie becoming the "o", the spoken warning and "the recruitment poster".
- **Rebuilt:** the body follows the dossier's 12-step progression. The viewer changes their mind with the player:
  *"we have to save this world"* → *"what does saving even mean?"* → *"how do we love people who are gone without
  stopping the lives of those who remain?"*. It is not a plot summary. Each block exists to overturn a belief that
  the previous block built.
- **Characters:** Gustave carries the motto's first meaning. Renoir appears three times (the old man, the painted
  husband, the father) and is never "the destroyer". Aline is a mother with a brush, never "the villain who couldn't
  let go". Maelle's argument gets her own words and the same length as Verso's.
- **Endings:** neither is crowned. They get mirrored sentences, mirrored costs and the same screen time. The verdict is
  handed to Esquie and Verso arguing about a rock.
- **Destination:** legacy and continuation, not "letting go" alone: *don't disappear with them*.
- **Life and humour** run through the whole script, not just the start: the recruitment poster, Esquie's rocks and
  napping, "the super nice one died", the piano at camp. They sit right before the hardest hits.

## Creative spine
- **Viewer question:** Why do millions of people love a game that takes away almost everyone you meet?
- **Hidden question:** How do you love someone who's gone without stopping the lives of the people still here?
- **Compass, never narrated as a list:** what we owe the dead · what we owe the living · what we owe those who come after.
- **Thesis:** Expedition 33 keeps changing what "saving" means until the only thing left worth saving is what people
  leave behind. Gustave knew that on page one of his journal. The Dessendres take a whole game to learn it, and so do you.
- **Payoff:** Gustave's last words were a *question* ("For those who come after, right?"). The video's last line is
  the same words with the question mark gone. The question has been answered, and "those who come after" now means
  everyone who has to wake up the next morning, which includes us.
- **Risk:** the reveal stretch turning into explanation (block E–F). It is kept to the facts that change a belief, and
  each one is tied to a face.

### A · Hook · spoiler-free
> FRAME 0 = the thumbnail, live: the close-up face, eyes brimming, the black tower over his shoulder with the "33" being painted stroke by stroke and a petal lifting off his palm. There is no establishing shot. Then a pull-back to the dusk harbor, where a woman is seen from behind. Far across the water, a huge round shape watches the tower (Esquie, unexplained).

**¶01** Once a year, a giant woman paints a number on a stone tower.

**¶02** Tomorrow, she paints thirty-three.

**¶03** And everyone who is thirty-three or older... turns to petals and drifts away.

**¶04** A man walks the woman he loves down to the harbor. She is thirty-three. He gets one last minute.

**¶05** That's how this game begins. Not with a tutorial. With a goodbye.

**¶06** It won Game of the Year at every major awards show. More than eight million copies sold.

**¶07** Millions of people paid... to lose everyone.

**¶08** So, why?

> The wordmark lands with its gold bar. Esquie rises behind it like a sunrise, flops forward and settles into the "o", beaming (COMPANION.md). 1.5 s of silence. Then the card FULL SPOILERS · INCLUDING BOTH ENDINGS.

### W · Warning

**¶09** One warning. The answer is in the ending, so we're talking about all of it. If you haven't finished it... this is your stop. We'll wait. Probably.

### B · The city that's used to it · step 1: Lumière, the Gommage, a clear enemy
> VISUAL BEAT (≈ 0:50): a Lumière street in the morning. Cafés, iron lamps, long coats, and no grey hair anywhere: a bench with nobody on it. Then Sophie's face at the harbor, calm. Music: "Lumière" (what we love).

**¶10** Start with the city. Lumière. Paris, around nineteen hundred. Iron lamps. Cafés. Long coats.

**¶11** And not a single grey hair.

**¶12** No grandparents. In Lumière, the oldest person you know is always the next one to go.

**¶13** The woman at the harbor is Sophie. She isn't angry. She says, word for word: "I've lived 33 good years. Look around."

**¶14** That's the strange part. The city isn't panicking. It even has a saying for it. "Tomorrow comes."

**¶15** And it has an enemy. The Paintress. Kill her, and the number stops.

**¶16** So every year, the ones with one year left sail out to try. Every expedition before this one has failed.

**¶17** Which is not a great line for the recruitment poster.

### C · Gustave's journal · step 2: Gustave, sacrifice, the motto's first meaning
> VISUAL BEAT (≈ 1:35): the beach. Gustave's face as the expedition is cut down: fear, not heroics. Then the night camp, where Lune holds his stare. "Lumière" fades out under ¶20.

**¶18** The man from the harbor is Gustave. An engineer. Thirty-two. One year left, and he's spending it on the boat.

**¶19** And the other side of the sea teaches him fast: brave and unafraid are not the same thing.

**¶20** The expedition lands. Within minutes, almost all of it is gone. Gustave falls apart. He tells Lune, a survivor, he's done.

**¶21** And Lune reminds him of their oath. "When one falls, we continue." Then she leans on one word. "Not if. When."

**¶22** It's not a promise that you'll survive. It's a promise that you won't be the end.

**¶23** So he keeps walking. And he meets Esquie.

> Esquie: huge, round, beaming. He tries to fit through a gap he will clearly not fit through. A small comic beat.

**¶24** A creature from a children's story. Enormous, gentle, missing his magic rocks, and perfecting, in his words, "the Art... of Napping."

**¶25** At night, Gustave writes in his journal. The monsters. The beauty of the continent. Riding a legend. It's for his apprentices back home. Kids who'll be on this boat one day.

> The journal page, close: his handwriting, the ink still wet on the sign-off. The words appear on the page and are not painted over the image.

**¶26** And he signs it off the same way.

**¶27** "For those who come after."

**¶28** That's what it means at the start. Not "we'll win." Something quieter. I don't need to live forever for my life to matter.

### D · The cliff and the rock · step 3: the mission, Gustave's death, life that goes on
> VISUAL BEAT (≈ 2:50): the old man with white hair, seen from a distance on a ridge, then closer each time. On the cliff we cut before any blow. Gustave in front of Maelle. His face. Her face. Then silence (2 s, no music).

**¶29** Now the story has a shape. A city to save. And an old man with white hair who keeps getting in the way.

**¶30** He was there on the beach, too.

**¶31** At the end of the first act, he catches them on a cliff. Gustave steps in front of Maelle, sixteen... and he doesn't get back up.

**¶32** His last words are to her. And they're a question. "For those who come after, right?"

**¶33** And the old man says something that makes no sense yet. "You won't yet understand. But this is a kindness, not a cruelty."

**¶34** Hold on to that.

> 2 s of silence. Then the funeral: Maelle's face, Lune's, Sciel's. Esquie sits a little apart, too big for the circle.

**¶35** At the funeral, Lune says the oath again. "When one falls, we continue."

**¶36** So you continue. A stranger named Verso joins, and the game hands you his sword instead.

**¶37** And Esquie, Verso's old friend, sums it up the way only Esquie can. "Well, the super nice one died but... the others are cool too."

**¶38** Then he tells Verso: "Losing a rock is better than never having a rock. Ask me how I know."

**¶39** Verso says: "Debatable."

**¶40** Remember that argument. The whole ending is that argument.

> VISUAL BEAT: camp at night. Verso at the piano; Maelle sits down beside him and plays too. Hold on their hands, not their faces. Esquie sways in the background. The only music is the piano in the scene; Bram's voice stays out until it settles.

**¶41** And there's still life. Verso at the piano. Maelle sits down and plays too. Nobody dies in that scene. People remember it anyway.

### E · The mission works · step 4 (certainty) → step 6 (the reveal, the retention pivot)
> VISUAL BEAT (≈ 4:15): the climb up the tower. The old man guarding the Paintress. Then silence, and the camera pulls back from Lumière... and back... until the city is a canvas on an easel in a dim Paris studio around 1900. A covered mirror. A child's sketch of Esquie pinned beside the easel (no narration).

**¶42** So you push on. Up the tower. The old man is guarding the Paintress. You beat him. You beat her.

**¶43** It worked. The mission Gustave died for... worked.

**¶44** And then the game takes it back.

**¶45** The Paintress is gone, and the Gommage doesn't stop. It takes everyone in Lumière. Everyone.

**¶46** Because she was never the one painting death. She was the one holding it back.

**¶47** And the world itself is a painting. Not a metaphor. An actual painting.

**¶48** A family of painters, the Dessendres, can paint worlds... and walk into them. Their son, Verso, painted this one. Then there was a fire. And Verso died.

### F · A mother with a brush · step 7 (Aline) + step 5 (Renoir, three times)
> Aline, in close-up: the face of a mother, not a monster. No villain lighting. "Une vie à t'aimer" enters on ¶52 (the quiet, vocal-led passage), the first time it is heard in the video.

**¶49** His mother, Aline, went to the last place her son still was. She walked into his painting. And she didn't come out.

**¶50** She became the Paintress. And inside, she painted her family back. A son. A husband.

**¶51** Call it madness. Then think of the room you still haven't cleared out. Aline just had a brush.

**¶52** And the old man on the cliff... was a painted husband. Made by a grieving woman to stand between her and anyone who'd take this away.

> VISUAL BEAT (≈ 5:20): back to the tower fight, now seen as a man shielding his wife. The music swells here, in the rest.

**¶53** Go back to that fight now. You weren't fighting a monster. You were fighting a marriage.

**¶54** And the real father, Renoir, came in to bring her home. The only way to do it... was to erase the painting. One year at a time.

**¶55** That's the Gommage. That's Sophie at the harbor.

**¶56** And he'd been the quiet figure helping you all along. Even with the weapon you used on the Paintress.

**¶57** To save the family he still had, he was destroying what was left of the son he lost.

**¶58** Nobody in this family thinks they're the villain. Even the man on the cliff called it a kindness.

### G · Two siblings · step 8: Verso and Maelle, equal arguments
> Maelle's face, then Verso's, framed identically (same lens, same light, same length on screen). The music continues, lower.

**¶59** And then it gets closer.

**¶60** Maelle, the girl Gustave died for, is Alicia. The little sister from the fire. Reborn in the painting, with no memory.

**¶61** Out there, she's a scarred girl in a house full of grief. In here, she had Gustave. Friends. A life.

**¶62** Her case isn't selfish. "This lifetime that was stolen from us."

**¶63** And Verso isn't the real son. He's a painted one, held together by the last piece of the real one. And that piece wants to stop.

**¶64** His is even shorter. "I don't want this life."

**¶65** One wants to stay where she finally belongs. One is begging to be allowed to end. Both are right about something.

> "Une vie à t'aimer" resolves into silence on ¶65. The choice is voice only.

### H · The brush · step 9: the two endings, mirrored
> VISUAL BEAT (≈ 6:50): the brush held out to the camera, to the viewer. Two doors in the studio. Each ending gets the same screen time and the same framing.

**¶66** At the end, you choose.

> Door one: Lumière rebuilt, faces coming home, the concert. Then Maelle's face, changing as she stays (faithful moment 6).

**¶67** Maelle's way. The painting stays. Lumière is rebuilt. Its people come back. Gustave. Sophie. Everyone the petals took... home.

**¶68** If you'd lost someone, and somebody handed you that brush... you'd think about it.

**¶69** But she paints Verso back too. He didn't ask to be. And the longer she stays, the more it costs her.

> Door two: the canvas fading; Lune's stare; Sciel. Then Paris, morning: a family at a small grave (faithful moment 7).

**¶70** Verso's way. The painting is erased, and everyone in it goes too. Lune. Sciel. Every camp. Every song. In Paris, a family finally stands together at a grave.

**¶71** Because a whole world is gone. One you lived in for thirty hours.

**¶72** One way saves the world, and costs the girl who's still alive. The other saves the girl, and costs the world.

**¶73** The game won't tell you which one is right. People are still arguing about it.

> Esquie and Verso at camp, from ¶38–¶39, replayed small and silent in the corner of the frame.

**¶74** Which is just Esquie and Verso again. "Losing a rock is better than never having a rock." "Debatable."

### I · What "saving" means · step 10
> The opening chalkboard / Emma's farewell podium in Lumière, empty. The word SAVE is not written on screen; the image does it.

**¶75** The story started with one simple sentence. We have to save this world.

**¶76** By the end, you're not sure what saving is. The painting? The people in it? The girl who has to wake up outside?

**¶77** Aline was saving a son. Renoir, a wife. Maelle, a family. Verso was trying to save everyone... from himself.

**¶78** Nobody here is wrong to love. The only question is what that love is allowed to stop.

### J · Tomorrow comes · step 11: legacy, continuation
> VISUAL BEAT (≈ 8:20): Gustave's journal again, on a table in morning light, closed. "For Those Who Come After" (official track, instrumental, RESEARCH addendum) enters low on ¶79.

**¶79** And that's where Gustave was ahead of all of them.

**¶80** He never tried to keep anything. He knew he had a year. He didn't ask the world to stop. He wrote it down, for kids he'd never see again.

**¶81** Aline couldn't let her son end, so she stopped everything. Gustave knew he'd end, and he kept going.

**¶82** Whichever door you pick, somebody wakes up the next morning. Maelle, in her painting. A family, in Paris. You, after the credits.

**¶83** Lumière already had the words. "Tomorrow comes."

**¶84** Some people leave us. What they gave us doesn't have to. A journal. A song at a piano. A rock you were lucky to have.

**¶85** You don't forget them. You just don't disappear with them.

### K · Payoff + motto + CTA · step 12
> The harbor at morning (a callback to the hook), empty, calm water. The music thins.

**¶86** So... why do millions of people love a game about losing everyone?

**¶87** Because it was never really about losing them. It's about the morning after.

**¶88** A man spent his last minute at a harbor. His last words were a question, to a girl who had to go on without him.

> The music resolves to silence. 2.5 s with no music and no voice. The journal opens by itself in the wind; a small hand (a child's, unidentified) turns to the last page. Esquie, at the frame edge, sets one rock on the table beside the journal and steps out of frame BEFORE the line (COMPANION.md: a witness, never on the line). The page shows the handwriting, which is only now visible: For those who come after.

**¶89** For those who come after.

> 1.5 s hold on the page, dry. Then the end card: the wordmark with an empty "o". Esquie walks up, looks at it, leaves his rock in it and steps out (book-end of the hook).

**¶90** So which ending did you choose? And what game deserves a "why?" next? Tell me in the comments.

**¶91** Maybe that's our next quest.

## Self-check: "Would someone who deeply loved Expedition 33 feel that we understood why it mattered?"
Mostly yes. The progression, the motto's arc and the even-handed endings are what fans argue about. These are the five
places where it may still fall short:
1. **Lune, Sciel and Monoco get only one line each.** Fans love them as people (Sciel's own grief, Lune's restraint, and
   Monoco is not mentioned at all). A fan may feel the party was reduced to Gustave + the Dessendres.
2. **Block F carries too much.** Aline, the painted husband, the father, the Gommage and the Curator's help all arrive in
   ~75 s. Someone who loved the Act 3 lore may find Renoir's turn (the man who "relents" and leaves with his wife) cut
   short. Clea is gone completely.
3. **Gameplay is nearly absent.** The dossier asks that viewers feel they *played* it (parry, the Monolith fight as a
   fight). Only ¶36 ("the game hands you his sword instead") and the tower fight touch it, and players who loved the combat
   may notice.
4. **Some loved moments rest on secondary or unverified sources:** the camp piano (fan wiki), the concert and Maelle's
   face (dossier), and the ES-419 motto. If any is off in-game, the people who know it best will catch it first.
5. **The legacy lines (¶84–¶85) are the most "general" writing in the script.** They are anchored by the journal, the
   piano and the rock, but a fan could still hear them as a greeting-card moral. The motto's silence has to do the
   rest, so the 2.5 s pause and the page must not be cut in the edit.
