# A · GTA VI

## Título y miniatura
1. **GTA 6: Why the Wait Became Part of the Game** ← recommended (44 characters). The hook now ends on the same idea and the payoff answers it literally ("the only part of GTA 6 anyone could play").
2. Why Have Millions Waited 13 Years for One Game?
3. We Got Everything Before GTA 6

**Thumbnail:** Quest (red hoodie, controller) in front of a wall calendar covered in thirteen years of red crosses, a comically long grey beard (gag prop; his face unchanged), deadpan stare. Text: **13 YEARS** (variants **STILL WAITING** / **BEFORE GTA 6**). No logo, no game art. The video opens on that calendar.

## Guion (EN, narración)

Since 2013, the world has had a pandemic. A new pope. A total solar eclipse. A congressional hearing about aliens.

And somewhere along the way, people started measuring all of it the same way.

Before GTA 6.

Thirteen years. Two trailers. And a forum with close to a million members... for a game none of them has played.

Waiting is supposed to be the empty part. The dead time between wanting something... and getting it. The loading screen of life.

But if you've been waiting since 2013, you know. This wait was never empty. Somehow, it became part of the game.

Why?

It started as a joke. In July 2023, right after a congressional hearing about unidentified flying objects, a TikTok said: "We gonna have aliens before GTA 6."

And the internet never stopped.

Every time the world did something enormous, someone added it to the list. Artificial intelligence? Before GTA 6.

It sounds like a complaint about a video game. But look at what it actually does. It turns the absence of one game into a calendar for real life.

GTA V came out in September 2013. One writer noted it came out six months after his son was born. That son is now in high school.

The game is still two trailers.

There is even a subreddit for GTA 6 that was created in September 2012. More than a year before GTA V came out. The sequel's fan club is older than the game it follows.

So the wait became a clock. But clocks only tick. And this wait was doing something else.

People were not sitting still. They were busy.

Busy watching two trailers. Not twice. Hundreds of times. Frame by frame. Zooming into a billboard in the background. Building entire theories out of a reflection in a car window. Some people have studied those two trailers harder than anything they ever studied in school.

When the first trailer leaked early, Rockstar released it that same night. In twenty-four hours, it passed ninety million views on YouTube. A Guinness world record for a game reveal. For a minute and a half of a game nobody could buy.

On that subreddit, people wear their waiting like a badge. One flair simply says: "Waiting for Nov 19th." Another: "Looking for the next trailer." Which is also a fair summary of their last few years.

Fans even learned to read Take-Two's earnings calls the way farmers read the sky. The last delay arrived on earnings day, in a sentence promising "the level of polish you have come to expect and deserve."

Which is a very elegant way of saying: not yet.

When a new trailer finally drops, the internet stops being an audience and becomes a forensic lab.

When there is almost no information, people start producing it themselves. The theories. The memes. The countdowns.

Waiting was not the delay before the hobby. Waiting was the hobby.

But why this game? People wait for sequels all the time. Most sequels arrive before anyone has time to miss them.

Part of the answer is that GTA V never left. Same game. Three generations of consoles. People bought it on one machine, then the next, then the next. The old city kept the lights on while the new one was being built.

And part of the answer is what GTA promises. Not a level. Not a story. A whole place. A city you could disappear into for years.

Which creates a strange situation. Because with so few trailers, nobody actually knows what that place is like.

So for thirteen years, everyone has been playing their own version of GTA 6. In their heads.

And that version is perfect. It has no bugs. No server queues. No price. It is exactly as big as you need it to be. And it runs beautifully on hardware that does not exist.

And everyone's version is slightly different. Which means that on launch day, millions of people will be disappointed by completely different things.

The imagined game has had a thirteen-year head start on the real one.

On the scheduled day, those two games finally meet. And only one of them has ever been perfect.

But that is not the biggest thing we are about to lose.

Think about how rare it is now for everyone to wait for the same thing. Your feed and mine have almost nothing in common.

Except one sentence.

A total solar eclipse happens, and somewhere in the comments, someone types three words. And a stranger on the other side of the world knows exactly what they mean.

Before GTA 6.

For thirteen years, that sentence was a place people met. A joke strangers could finish for each other.

So... why did the wait become part of the game?

Because for thirteen years, waiting was the only part of GTA 6 anyone could play.

And it was the only part we all played together.

"Before GTA 6" was never really about a game. It was a way of saying: a lot has happened to us. The world changed. And we were still here. Waiting. Together.

On November 19th, if the date holds, the waiting ends.

We finally get GTA 6.

What we lose... is "before GTA 6."

So what game have you been waiting for the longest? And what game deserves a "why?" next? Tell me in the comments.

Maybe that's our next quest.

# B · How to Fish

## Título y miniatura
1. **Why Did Millions Buy a Fishing Game... and Not Fish?** ← recommended (52 characters): a contradiction anyone gets in one read, and a question only the essay answers. Game name in the description, tags and on screen at 0:00.
2. How to Fish: The Game That Isn't About Fishing
3. Nobody Is Fishing in How to Fish

**Thumbnail:** Quest and three friends (Pixie + two friends) crammed into a tiny rowboat, laughing, a fishing rod tangled around everybody; one fish at the edge of the frame staring at the camera, unimpressed. Text: **NOT FISHING** (variants **WHY?!** / **JUST GET IT**). Our own style, no game art. The video opens on that boat.

## Guion (EN, narración)

There is a game called How to Fish. A boat. A rod. A lot of fish.

And almost nobody seems to be fishing.

They are slapping each other in the face. Gambling the fish money. Fishing with a sniper rifle.

It costs about eight dollars. Its makers hoped it might sell two hundred thousand copies. By early October, one analytics firm estimated more than ten million.

Millions of people bought a fishing game. To do everything else.

Together.

Why?

Start with the price. Eight dollars is not really a price. It's an invitation.

Because this is not really a game you play alone. Everyone in the boat buys their own copy. So it usually begins the same way. A message in a group chat. "It's eight dollars. Just get it."

And then, one by one: Bought. Bought. Bought.

And one friend who says "I'll get it later." He will not get it later.

Now compare that with the other way adults try to see their friends.

"We should hang out."
"Yes! Definitely."
"I'm free in three weeks."
"Three weeks works."

And three weeks later, somebody's kid is sick. Somebody's car is making a new noise. And the plan quietly dies in a chat nobody will ever scroll back to.

As an adult, the hard part of seeing your friends is not distance. It isn't even time. It's asking. Proposing a plan means admitting you want one.

Kids never had this problem. A kid just shows up at your door and says: "Want to play?" At some point, we lost that sentence. Nobody ever told us what replaced it.

Eight dollars removes the asking.

But why fishing? Of all the things a game could be about.

Because fishing solves a second problem. Not how to meet. What to do once you're there.

Ask anyone what they caught on their last fishing trip. Most don't remember. They remember who was in the boat.

In a boat, nobody has to look at each other. Nobody has to fill the silence. You both look at the water. You talk. Or you don't, and that is fine too. The fish gives the afternoon a reason... and the silence a job.

And fishing is not the only alibi adults have invented. The golf course. The poker night. The car that needs one more afternoon in the garage. Nobody in those places is really there for the golf, the cards or the car. Every generation has built its own excuse to sit next to each other.

How to Fish is just the newest one. It fits in a group chat.

And like every good alibi, it comes with props. A boat. A rod. A goal.

And then it gets rid of it almost immediately.

You begin with simple tools. Soon you have heavy weapons. You sell your catch. You gamble the money. You fight bosses. The developers' own description on Steam reads, word for word: "cartoon violence towards fish and gambling with fish... (no real money)." 

The goal is still there. But the goal is not the point. The goal is the alibi.

And what happens instead of fishing... is mostly failure.

Someone falls in the water. Someone finds a weapon that was clearly not designed for fish. Someone gambles the savings, and nobody will ever admit who it was. Someone gets launched across the island by something they absolutely should not have poked.

And when a teammate goes down, the way you bring them back is by picking them up... and slapping them in the face.

Which, if we're honest, is how most friendships handle feelings.

Nobody retells the time everything went perfectly. They retell the night it all went wrong. Usually years later, in the same group chat, with no context, to the confusion of everyone who wasn't there.

The internet even invented a name for games like this. Friendslop. Half a joke, half an insult. Cheap, chaotic co-op games built for playing with friends.

We named a whole genre after the word for garbage. Then we invited our friends to it.

Some say it's short. Maybe it is just this year's summer hit.

But fishing trips end too. That was never what they were for.

Some games just need to get a few friends into the same boat on a Tuesday night. The plan was to fish. The plan is always to fish.

And at some point, around one in the morning, someone says "last round." It is never the last round.

And the two friends who founded the studio behind it? They met in high school. Their first paid game had not done well. So they made a small game about friends doing something silly together.

And millions of people answered the same group chat.

So... why did millions of people buy a fishing game and not fish?

Because nobody in that boat came for the fish.

The fish was just the thing you could say out loud.

How to Fish sounds like a tutorial. And maybe it is.

Just not for fishing.

It teaches the thing adults are worst at.

How to say "I miss you"... without ever having to say it.

So who would you put in your boat? And what game deserves a "why?" next? Tell me in the comments.

Maybe that's our next quest.

# C · Bongo Cat

## Título y miniatura
1. **Why Do So Many Players Keep a Cat Watching Them Type?** ← recommended (52 characters; evergreen, no fluctuating number; "watching" is the irony the essay pays off).
2. Bongo Cat: The Game Where Nothing Happens
3. The Strange Reason Players Leave a Cat on Their Screen

**Thumbnail:** Quest at his desk at night, lit by the monitor, typing; a tiny original round cat at the bottom of the screen mid-slap; Quest glancing down at it with a small, tired smile. Text: **MY COWORKER** (variants **IT WATCHES** / **3 A.M. BUDDY**). An original cat with its own silhouette, never the game's sprite or the 2018 meme art.

## Guion (EN, narración)

On an ordinary day, well over a hundred thousand accounts are running a game in which nothing happens.

A small cat sits at the bottom of your screen. It's called Bongo Cat. Every time you press a key, it slaps your taskbar.

That's it. That's the game.

There is no story. No enemies. No ending. It does not even ask you to look at it. It might be the only game in history that is happy you're doing something else. You can't lose. You can't win. You can only... type.

And yet people leave it running for hours. Days.

Right there, at the edge of their screen, while they work.

Why?

The obvious answer is the hats.

Bongo Cat is free. While you type and click, you earn points, and points unlock hats for your cat. Some are common. Some are rare. The legendary ones, according to the game's own published odds, drop once in five hundred thousand.

And the items can be traded. So, technically, your cat is a small investment portfolio wearing a crown.

Notice what that does. You don't play to earn hats. You do whatever you were already doing. Emails. Spreadsheets. Homework. The work you had to do anyway quietly becomes the grind.

Your inbox becomes a slot machine. The quarterly report is now a loot box.

But that explanation has a problem. Because a lot of people forget the cat is even there. They leave it running behind other games, behind work, behind everything. Sometimes they only notice it when they glance down.

And that glance is the interesting part.

Most screen work isn't just invisible. It's lonely. Eight hours. One window. Nobody else in the room.

For a lot of people, working from home turned the office into a room with one person in it. No small talk. No coffee machine. The commute is a hallway. The coworker is a plant. And the plant is not great at conversation.

Bongo Cat puts something at the edge of that window. Something that moves when you move.

Type slowly, and it taps along. Stop to think, and it stops too, like it's thinking with you. Write an angry email, and you look down to find the cat absolutely losing its mind on your behalf. It does not judge the email. It simply agrees, at full speed.

And late at night, when the whole house is asleep, the only other thing still moving... is a paw.

The game's own Steam page calls it, in five words: "A friend while working hard."

Which sounds sweet. Until you learn how little that friend can actually do.

The cat does not know what you are typing. It does not know if you are working or wasting time. It does not even know if you are a person. In 2025, the developer estimated that maybe half of its players were bots.

Which means somewhere out there, right now, a cat is slapping a taskbar for nobody at all.

Bongo Cat was a meme long before this game existed. In 2018, someone drew a cat-like blob hitting a table, and someone else put bongos and music under it. Back then, you watched the cat.

Now the cat watches you. Sort of.

And the game did not even start as a cat. The very first prototype was a drill, digging into a stone.

The cat came later.

So by any reasonable measure, nobody is really watching you. And yet it still works.

Because it doesn't need to be real company. It only needs to slap the taskbar at the exact moment you hit send.

And then the developers added something that changes the picture.

Since August 2025, up to a hundred players' cats can share one lobby. A hundred taskbars.

Somewhere it is three in the morning, and someone is finishing a thesis. Somewhere it is lunch, and someone is pretending to answer emails. Somewhere, someone is just... typing.

Some of them are probably scripts. But plenty of them are people, each at their own desk, in their own time zone, in their own quiet room.

And for a moment, a hundred cats are tapping together.

Nobody in that lobby can see what the others are writing.

It is a little like an office, really. You never talk to the person at the next desk either. But you would notice if they left.

That's all a lobby like this really is. A hundred people agreeing, without a word, to keep going for a little longer.

But every paw that moves says the same thing.

Someone else is still here.

So... why do so many people keep a cat watching them type?

Not for the hats. Not really.

For most of the day, the only thing moving in the room is you.

Bongo Cat cannot really see you. It never could.

But at the bottom of your screen, a small paw moves when you move. Like it's saying...

I saw that.

So what game keeps you company? And what game deserves a "why?" next? Tell me in the comments.

Maybe that's our next quest.
